Slow start

Before the protocol, let the body find one safe moment.

If you are here after harm, you do not have to read everything or decide everything. Start with one breath, one object you can see, one person or resource that can help. Mountains, clouds, trees, wildflowers, and steady beauty can be gentle anchors while you choose the next right step.

01

Look for green.

Find a tree, plant, mountain, cloud, or image of nature. Let your eyes rest there for a few seconds.

02

Lengthen the exhale.

Try one softer breath out. No performance. Just a signal to your body that this minute can slow down.

The core correction

Not a reporting funnel. Not awareness-only. A protocol for changing risk environments.

SA/DV prevention requires more than a hotline, a vault, a compliance training, or an after-the-fact reporting pathway. The solution has to change the settings where harm becomes likely, tolerated, hidden, repeated, or poorly interrupted. Survivor-controlled response remains essential, but it sits inside a larger prevention system.

Evidence-based prevention protocol

Seven practices that fit together.

The protocol starts with CDC's public health approach and turns research into operational practice for schools, sports, workplaces, campuses, clinics, and coalitions.

01

Map the risk environment.

Identify where harm becomes more likely: dating relationships, teams, schools, workplaces, campuses, nightlife, digital spaces, rural isolation, economic dependency, housing insecurity, coercive control, retaliation, and alcohol-context settings.

CDC public health step: define and monitor the problem.
02

Install protective infrastructure.

Train trusted adults and peers, build warm referral loops, improve policies, strengthen school and workplace climate, support housing and economic stability, and create safe disclosure channels.

CDC SV/IPV strategies: social norms, skills, protective environments, economic supports.
03

Make public places response-ready.

Put SAFE instruction packets near AEDs and first-aid stations in schools, libraries, campuses, arenas, transit hubs, courthouses, and public buildings: hotline access, nearest SAFE/SANE provider, evidence-preservation basics, and private QR routing.

Boundary: instructions and routing, not public-building forensic self-collection.
04

Interrupt escalation earlier.

Build pathways for stalking, coercive control, repeat boundary violations, harassment clusters, digital abuse, threats, isolation, retaliation, and alcohol-enabled exploitation before a crisis becomes the first point of contact.

Practice aim: reduce severity, recurrence, and missed warning signs.
05

Support survivors without coercion.

Provide immediate resources, safety planning, medical and legal orientation, evidence preservation education, and advocate-supported choices without forcing a police, Title IX, or institutional reporting path.

Boundary: survivor-facing data serves the survivor first.
06

Detect repeat harm safely.

Use optional, privacy-preserving matching and pattern detection only with clear consent, minimization, survivor control, and advocate support. Repeat-harm claims need nuance.

Informed by Project Callisto; supported cautiously by PMID 11991158 and PMID 26168230.
07

Evaluate what reduces harm.

Measure norms, bystander behavior, early help-seeking, time to support, survivor-reported control, repeat harm, institutional response quality, housing stabilization, and implementation fidelity.

CDC public health step: develop, test, adopt, and continuously assess.
08

Govern the system.

Require survivor advisory governance, privacy/security review, clinical/legal review, local community input, Tribal partnership by invitation, and a strict evidence standard for public claims.

Rule: PMID for research claims; official guidance labeled separately.

CDC risk and protective factors

Target environments, not blame.

CDC frames sexual violence as shaped by factors across individual, relationship, community, and societal levels. These are prevention signals, not a way to label any person as destined to harm others or to blame survivors.

Risk factors may increase likelihood but may not be direct causes. Protective factors may reduce likelihood. The practical use is to design safer systems, stronger norms, and earlier intervention points.

Individual risk factors
  • Alcohol and drug use
  • Delinquency
  • Lack of concern for others
  • Aggressive behaviors and acceptance of violent behaviors
  • Early sexual initiation
  • Coercive sexual fantasies
  • Preference for impersonal sex and sexual risk-taking
  • Exposure to sexually explicit media
  • Hostility toward women
  • Adherence to traditional social norms about how men and women behave
  • Hyper-masculinity
  • Suicidal behavior
  • Prior sexual victimization or perpetration
Relationship risk factors
  • Family history of conflict and violence
  • Childhood history of physical, sexual, or emotional abuse
  • Emotionally unsupportive family environment
  • Poor parent-child relationships, particularly with fathers
  • Association with sexually aggressive, hyper-masculine, and delinquent peers
  • Involvement in a violent or abusive intimate relationship
Community risk factors
  • Poverty
  • Lack of employment opportunities
  • Lack of institutional support from police and judicial systems
  • General tolerance of sexual violence within the community
  • Weak community sanctions against sexual violence perpetrators
Societal risk factors
  • Societal norms that support sexual violence
  • Societal norms that support male superiority and sexual entitlement
  • Societal norms that maintain women's inferiority and sexual submissiveness
  • Weak laws and policies related to sexual violence and fairness between men and women
  • High levels of crime and other forms of violence
  • Negative attitudes or beliefs against groups due to race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, sex, disability, social class, or country of origin
  • Stigma against people who exchange sex for money or resources

Protective factors for perpetration

Build the opposite conditions.

The SAFE Standard should increase protective factors while reducing risks: emotional health, connectedness, academic achievement, empathy, and family conflict resolution.

  • Emotional health and connectedness
  • Academic achievement
  • Empathy and concern for how one's actions affect others
  • Families where caregivers work through conflicts peacefully

Source: CDC Risk and Protective Factors. Primary review: PMID 23275472.

CDC prevention resource for action

Turn risk-factor data into prevention levers.

CDC's prevention framework points to five strategy families. Prevention OS translates them into a practical operating model for schools, campuses, workplaces, sports programs, clinics, public buildings, and community coalitions.

Strategy 01

Promote social norms that protect against violence.

  • Bystander approaches.
  • Mobilizing men and boys as allies.

Apply this as peer-led norms campaigns, coach and supervisor scripts, visible anti-retaliation commitments, and repeated practice in how to interrupt harm safely.

Strategy 02

Teach skills to prevent sexual violence.

  • Social-emotional learning.
  • Healthy, safe dating and intimate relationship skills for adolescents.
  • Healthy sexuality.
  • Empowerment-based training.

Apply this as age-appropriate consent, respect, conflict, digital-boundary, and help-seeking practice before risk peaks, not only after an incident.

Strategy 03

Empower and support girls and women.

  • Strengthening economic supports for women and families.
  • Strengthening leadership and opportunities for girls.

Apply this through financial stability, housing navigation, leadership pathways, transport, childcare, and resources that reduce dependency on unsafe people or institutions.

Strategy 04

Create protective environments.

  • Improving safety and monitoring in schools.
  • Consistently applying workplace policies.
  • Addressing community-level risks through environmental approaches.

Apply this by changing place-based risk: lighting, staffing, transport, supervision, private help access, nightlife partnerships, housing policy, and consequences for repeat harm.

Strategy 05

Support victims and survivors to lessen harms.

  • Victim-centered services.
  • Treatment for victims of sexual violence.
  • Treatment for at-risk children and families to prevent problem behavior, including sex offending.

Apply this as the safety net: advocate access, SAFE/SANE routing, survivor-controlled documentation, trauma treatment, family services, and no-wrong-door support.

Minimum robust intervention

Pair one place-based safety upgrade, one skills/norms program, one survivor-controlled response pathway, and one measurement dashboard. That is the seatbelt-level unit: small enough to deploy, broad enough to change risk.

Evaluation spine

Track adoption, fidelity, climate, bystander confidence, time to support, survivor-reported control, repeat-harm signals, and referral completion. Do not claim prevention without measurement.

Public health framing

Basile and Smith's CDC-authored review frames sexual violence victimization of women as a prevalence, impact, and prevention problem for public health. Use it to explain why the site connects survivor support with population-level prevention.

Novel infrastructure pilots

Make the day after survivable, practical, and free of avoidable barriers.

These are not substitutes for prevention. They are public-health readiness systems that reduce secondary harm when prevention fails.

Public building SAFE readiness

Put sexual-assault response instructions where communities already put emergency instructions.

AED cabinets teach bystanders what to do in a cardiac emergency. Public buildings could also carry a sealed SAFE response card: call/text options, RAINN hotline, nearest sexual assault forensic exam provider, local advocate contacts, evidence-preservation basics, and a private QR code that opens a survivor-controlled resource page.

AED-adjacent SAFE station could include: Sealed response card RAINN/local hotline QR codes Nearest SAFE/SANE site Printed survivor choice bag

Forensic access network

Train more nurses and first-contact sites.

Hospitals remain central, but the access network can include trained nurses, mobile SANE/SART response, tele-advocate support, and fire stations or EMS-linked locations that know how to stabilize, protect privacy, and route survivors to qualified forensic exam care.

No-cost pharmacy bridge

Emergency contraception should not become a second punishment.

Build a pharmacy access pilot with Walgreens, CVS, independent pharmacies, clinics, and victim compensation funds: no-cost emergency contraception for recent rape survivors, stock audits, private checkout flows, and advocate-issued access codes where legally permissible.

Aftercare, not one-and-done

The response has to continue after the first visit.

Follow-up should include STI care, pregnancy-related options, mental health support, housing and safety planning, school/work protections, legal orientation, and survivor-controlled documentation updates.

Survivor-controlled response safety net

The vault is not the whole solution. It is what protects agency when harm occurs.

Project Callisto demonstrates a smart safety-net model: encrypted, timestamped documentation and optional matching with others harmed by the same person. Prevention OS uses that idea as one carefully governed layer inside a broader public-health protocol.

For friends and family

How to help without taking over.

Trauma can affect thinking, behavior, and memory in ways that are often misunderstood. Jim Hopper's work on sexual assault and the brain emphasizes survival-mode responses, reflexes and habits, fragmented memory, and the importance of reducing shame. Loved ones can make the next hours and weeks safer by offering calm, choice, and steady support.

Sexual assault and the brain Healing resources

Say this first.

"I believe you." "You are not to blame." "You do not have to decide everything right now." "I can stay with you or help you reach an advocate."

Offer choices.

Ask before touching, calling, driving, documenting, or telling anyone. Offer two or three concrete options rather than a flood of advice.

Understand survival responses.

Not fighting, not yelling, delayed disclosure, calm affect, confusion, or fragmented memory do not mean nothing happened.

Avoid these harms.

Do not interrogate, blame, demand a report, pressure forgiveness, post online, confront the person accused, or make the survivor manage your emotions.

Keep showing up.

Help with food, transport, childcare, appointments, safe housing, school or work logistics, and quiet check-ins after the first wave of crisis passes.

Bring in trained support.

Offer RAINN, a local advocate, SANE/SART care, campus advocacy, or DV support. Let trained helpers carry what friends and family should not carry alone.
